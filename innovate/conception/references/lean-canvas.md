# Lean Canvas

The Lean Canvas states the whole business model as nine hypotheses on one page. Every block
is a claim to be validated, never a fact. Use it late in conception, once the customer, job,
alternatives, and solution fit are established — it consolidates them into a business model
and produces the tagged assumption set the validation milestone works from.

## The nine blocks

| # | Block | The question | Its output |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Problem | What are the top three problems? | Problem statements in customer language |
| 2 | Customer segments | Who experiences them? | Primary segment plus early adopters |
| 3 | Unique value proposition | Why different and worth attention? | One sentence |
| 4 | Solution | What are the top three features? | A feature-to-problem mapping |
| 5 | Channels | How will customers be reached? | Acquisition and delivery channels |
| 6 | Revenue streams | How is money made? | A pricing-model hypothesis |
| 7 | Cost structure | What are the main costs? | Fixed vs. variable |
| 8 | Key metrics | How is success measured? | Three to five stage-appropriate metrics |
| 9 | Unfair advantage | What cannot be easily copied? | A defensibility thesis |

## Block guidance

**Problem** — customer language, not feature gaps; include the consequence of not solving it;
treat "do nothing" as an implicit alternative.

**Customer segments** — the primary segment by role, organization type and size, and
geography; early adopters are the narrower slice feeling the pain most acutely; secondary
segments are marked "future" only; the definition must be specific enough to name three real
examples.

**Unique value proposition** — the formula is: "For [segment] who struggle with [problems],
[product] is a [category] that [benefit], unlike [alternatives] which [shortcoming]." It
states the change in the customer's world, not the product's features, and must differentiate
against at least two named alternatives.

**Solution** — three to five features that deliver the value proposition, each mapped to a
specific problem or job, each expressed as what changes for the customer.

**Channels** — discovery channels (how they first hear) separated from activation channels
(how they first experience value); matched to the behaviours already mapped; self-serve or
sales-assisted according to the segment.

**Revenue streams** — a pricing model suited to how the segment actually buys (per-seat,
usage-based, freemium, transaction fee), aligned to the value created, with budget authority
and procurement path noted.

**Cost structure** — fixed (salaries, core infrastructure, compliance) vs. variable (hosting
per user, per-call costs, support load), with the step functions named ("hire support at
twenty customers") and the costs that drive unit economics flagged.

**Key metrics** — three to five tied to the job and the value proposition, each classified
leading (early signal) or lagging (outcome), each measurable at the current stage. No vanity
metrics: raw signups without context is not a metric.

**Unfair advantage** — must be hard to copy AND hard to buy. Valid: proprietary data, network
effects, distribution partnerships, deep domain expertise. Rejected: "great team", "first
mover", and any generic advantage.

## Assumption tagging

Every block's claims are tagged so the validation milestone can pick them up — the source
convention uses a per-block prefix and a number: P1–P3 (problem), CS1–CS2 (customer
segments), UVP1, SOL1, CH1–CH2, REV1, COST1, MET1, UA1. Each tag carries a falsifiable
statement, for example "problem occurs more than twice per week", "customers will pay $X per
seat", "infrastructure costs stay under 20% of revenue".

## What a good output contains

- All nine blocks populated; a canvas brief defining scope, segment, and stage; a version tag
  (for example "Lean Canvas v0.1 — idea stage").
- Traceability: each problem traces to Jobs-to-be-Done or Problem-Solution Fit; each solution
  element traces to a problem; the value proposition is consistent with the press-release
  headline; the unfair advantage is informed by Competitive Landscape.
- At least three assumptions tagged for validation, and none marked validated without
  evidence.
- A prioritization naming the three riskiest assumptions with the consequence of each being
  wrong — this is what the validation milestone starts from.

## Builds on / feeds

Builds on all of Working Backwards (customer, press-release headline), Jobs-to-be-Done (job
forces, job owners), Competitive Landscape (unfair advantage), and Problem-Solution Fit
(problem brief, solution space, constraints). Owns customer segments and the business-model
structure and is the terminal framework for the business model — nothing later in the trail
redefines it; Five Whys refines its Problem block and Key Metrics, and the validation
milestone consumes its tagged assumptions.
