# Competitive Landscape

Understanding competitors is not about copying features — it is about understanding how the
market currently addresses the customer's job, including the customers who address it by
doing nothing. Use it after Working Backwards and Jobs-to-be-Done, once the customer and the
job are defined: before a solution can be claimed to fit better, the existing alternatives,
what works in them, what does not, and where the gaps sit must be known.

> Research rigor — how a source is found, evaluated, scored, and cited — is NOT this
> reference's subject; it lives in `../../../web/research/references/standards.md`. This
> reference carries the business-analysis frame only. Claims here must come from current
> research, never from a model's training data.

## The sequence

Worked in this order, always. Each step consumes the previous one's output, so a skipped or
reordered step is not a shortcut — it silently invalidates everything downstream of it. Steps 2–6
are the five analysis dimensions described in the next section, in the order they must be worked.

| # | Step | What it produces | What breaks if it is skipped or taken out of order |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Confirm the prerequisites | The customer and problem from Working Backwards and the primary job from Jobs-to-be-Done, restated in one line each | "Competitor" has no definition, so the analysis collects companies that look similar instead of options that serve the same job |
| 2 | Map the current alternatives | Direct competitors, indirect alternatives, and the non-consumption cases with the reason each customer does nothing | Doing nothing — the most common alternative — never enters the landscape, and the venture is compared only against software |
| 3 | Benchmark geographically | Two structurally different markets compared on players, maturity, business models and regulation, each with the lesson for this venture's own context | Patterns already proven elsewhere are re-derived from scratch, and a regulatory constraint that decides feasibility is found late |
| 4 | Find the cross-industry analogues | The core problem pattern abstracted, plus where it is already solved in other industries and what transfers | The solution space stays inside one category, which is where the differentiation candidates are thinnest |
| 5 | Deep-dive each major competitor | Strengths, weaknesses, target customer, pricing, go-to-market, technology dependencies and funding stage per competitor | Step 7's opportunities have no evidence under them, because shared competitor weakness is what makes an opportunity real |
| 6 | Plot the positioning map | The two dimensions that matter most to the customer, every alternative plotted, and the unoccupied quadrants named | White space is asserted rather than shown, and the differentiation claim rests on nothing visible |
| 7 | Rate the threats | At least five threats, each with a severity rating and a mitigation | The analysis reads as an opportunity pitch; the reasons this fails are never written down |
| 8 | Rate the opportunities | At least five opportunities scored on impact and feasibility, with the top three to five selected to inform solution design | Everything found stays unranked, and the next framework inherits a list rather than a direction |
| 9 | Build the assumption inventory | At least ten assumptions, each with a category, a confidence level and a validation method, of which three to five are flagged high-risk | Findings drawn from research are carried forward as facts, and the validation milestone has nothing specific to test |

Steps 3 and 4 are the one permitted exception: they are independent of each other and may be worked
in either order, or in parallel. Nothing else moves.

## The five analysis dimensions

**1. Current alternatives.** Direct competitors (same problem, same customer); indirect
alternatives (other categories serving the same job — spreadsheets, manual process,
consultants); non-consumption (why some customers do nothing: awareness, cost, complexity,
inertia).

**2. Geographic benchmarking.** Compare a mature market against a structurally different one
(the source framework uses the US and China) across: leading players and their share;
solution maturity and trajectory; business models; regulatory environment; and, for each, the
lesson for this venture's own context — what can be imported, adapted, or avoided.

**3. Cross-industry analogues.** Abstract the core problem pattern and find where it is
already solved elsewhere. Examples of the move: asset liquidity → auto finance, invoice
factoring, art-backed lending; matching or marketplace → dating, job boards, real estate;
compliance and verification → know-your-customer, identity verification, background checks;
subscription management → software, media streaming, fitness.

**4. Competitor deep-dive.** Per major competitor: strengths (product, distribution, brand,
team); weaknesses (product gaps, service, limitations); target customer (segment, size,
geography); pricing model; go-to-market (sales, product-led, partnerships); technology and
platform dependencies; funding stage and growth trajectory.

**5. Competitive positioning.** Pick the TWO dimensions that matter most to the customer —
price vs. feature depth, specialization vs. breadth, self-service vs. high-touch, small
business vs. enterprise, speed vs. accuracy, simplicity vs. power — plot every alternative,
and identify the unoccupied quadrants as candidate white space.

## What a good output contains

Seven sections: current alternatives; geographic benchmarking with lessons; cross-industry
analogues and their transferable insights; competitor deep-dives; the positioning map and
feature comparison; threats and opportunities; and the assumption inventory.

The quality bar from the source's synthesis step:

- At least **five specific threats**, each with a severity rating and a mitigation, drawn
  across incumbent advantages, emerging players, platform risk, geographic entry,
  cross-industry disruption, technology shifts, and regulatory change.
- At least **five opportunities**, each rated on impact and feasibility, drawn from shared
  competitor weaknesses, underserved segments, unmet jobs, geographic lessons not yet
  applied, cross-industry patterns not yet imported, positioning white space, and technology
  the competitors have not adopted — with the top three to five selected to inform solution
  design.
- At least **ten assumptions** documented with a category (competitive, market, geographic,
  cross-industry, customer), a confidence level, and a concrete validation method — of which
  three to five are flagged high-risk because being wrong about them would invalidate the
  differentiation strategy.

## Pitfalls

Answering from training data instead of current research. Ignoring non-traditional
competitors and workarounds. Ignoring geographic context. Stopping at a feature list instead
of analyzing strengths, weaknesses, and positioning. Stating a differentiation claim without
tagging it as an assumption. Producing analysis that connects to no opportunity and no
validation work.

## Builds on / feeds

Builds on Working Backwards (customer and problem) and Jobs-to-be-Done (the job and its
current alternatives) — both are prerequisites. Owns competitive positioning and the market
alternatives: Problem-Solution Fit takes the available solutions, alternatives, and
constraints from here; Lean Canvas references it for the Unfair Advantage, Channels, and
competition blocks; the validation milestone's Leap of Faith takes the competitive
assumptions and its Pre-mortem the competitive threats. For structured product-feature
comparison across a set of benchmark documents, the sibling `benchmark-analysis.md` is the
technique; this framework owns the market facts and the positioning.
