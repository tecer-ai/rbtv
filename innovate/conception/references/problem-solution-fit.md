# Problem-Solution Fit

The Problem-Solution Fit canvas tests whether a solution genuinely fits a specific customer
problem in a concrete context, rather than being a plausible-sounding answer to an abstract
problem category. Use it after Working Backwards and Jobs-to-be-Done, once a customer and a
job exist, and before Lean Canvas — it is what turns a solution narrative into a solution
that can be defended element by element.

## The sequence

Worked in this order, always. Each step consumes the previous one's output, so a skipped or
reordered step is not a shortcut — it silently invalidates everything downstream of it. The canvas
is filled problem-space first and solution last, and that direction is the whole point of it.

| # | Step | What it produces | What breaks if it is skipped or taken out of order |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Lock the scope | ONE named primary segment and ONE recurring situation, written down before anything is filled in | The canvas drifts across customer types, and every block afterwards describes an average customer who does not exist |
| 2 | Write the problem-space brief | Who the customer is and the context they are in, carried from Working Backwards and Jobs-to-be-Done | The canvas restates the venture's own framing instead of the customer's, and nothing later can be checked against a customer |
| 3 | Fill problem, triggers and emotions | The recurring pain as experienced in situ, when and why customers act, and the emotional state before and after | Without the trigger the solution has no moment to attach to, and without the emotions only the functional job survives |
| 4 | List the available solutions | Existing alternatives and the specific drawback of each | Step 7's solution has nothing to improve on, so "better" cannot be stated as anything measurable |
| 5 | Map behaviour and its channels | The coping strategies and workarounds in use, and where the problem-related actions actually happen | The workarounds people already pay for in time stay invisible, and the adoption path is designed for a workflow nobody uses |
| 6 | Surface the customer constraints | All four kinds — financial, time and attention, skills and tools, organizational — stated at full strength | A constraint discovered after the solution is written gets quietly softened to keep the solution intact |
| 7 | Write the solution | The solution concept, not a feature list, with every element traced to a behaviour it replaces, an alternative it improves on, or a constraint it respects | Written before steps 3–6 it becomes the thing the problem is fitted to, which is the exact failure this canvas exists to catch |
| 8 | Run the alignment check | An explicit statement, element by element, of what each solution element traces to — and the removal of any element that traces to nothing | Untraceable features stay on the canvas and are carried into Lean Canvas as if they had been validated |
| 9 | Tag the critical assumptions | Every "this assumes that…" belief classified behavioural, technical or economic, prioritized by the impact of being wrong, each paired with a test that would falsify it — with the top three to five genuinely uncomfortable | The validation milestone inherits a canvas that reads as established fact, and Leap of Faith has nothing to take |
| 10 | Write the synthesis | Roughly 250 words naming what was established, what remains a gap, and what is handed to the next framework | The next framework re-reads the whole canvas and picks its own starting point, usually a different one |

Nothing here may be worked out of order — in particular, the solution is never written before the
problem space is complete.

## The ten canvas blocks

| Block | What it locks down | Where its material comes from |
|---|---|---|
| Problem-space brief | Who, and in what context | Working Backwards + Jobs-to-be-Done |
| Problem | The recurring pain as experienced, in situ | Discovery questioning |
| Triggers | When and why customers act | The struggling moments from Jobs-to-be-Done |
| Emotions, before and after | The functional and emotional jobs | The press-release narrative + Jobs-to-be-Done |
| Available solutions | Existing alternatives and their drawbacks | Market observation |
| Behaviour | Coping strategies and workarounds | Customer research |
| Channels of behaviour | Where problem-related actions actually happen | Workflow mapping |
| Customer constraints | Limits on adoption | Budget, skills, organizational politics |
| Our solution | The solution concept — not a feature list | The press release and FAQ solution |
| Critical assumptions | Tagged beliefs requiring validation | The internal FAQ plus this analysis |

## The three rules the canvas enforces

**Scope.** ONE primary segment and ONE recurring situation per canvas. Never cover multiple
customer types in one canvas — when a second segment emerges, split it into its own canvas.

**Solution traceability.** Never jump from problem to feature backlog. Every solution element
must trace back to one of three things: a behaviour it replaces or augments, an alternative
it improves on, or a constraint it respects or relaxes. An element that traces to none of
them does not belong on the canvas.

**Constraint honesty.** Never downplay a constraint. Surface all four kinds: financial
(budget, cost structure); time and attention (competing priorities); skills and tools
(technical literacy); organizational (approvals, compliance, security review).

## Assumptions

Every belief the canvas rests on is written as a "this assumes that…" statement, classified
as **behavioural** (the customer experiences the problem at a given frequency; the customer
is willing to change behaviour), **technical** (feasibility and performance), or **economic**
(pricing, costs, market size, buying process); prioritized by the impact of being wrong; and
paired with a concrete test or signal that would falsify it.

## What a good output contains

- All ten blocks populated, with the problem-space brief, the problem/triggers/emotions
  group, the behaviours and constraints group, the solution, and the critical assumptions
  each explicitly checked as complete or not.
- An alignment check: the solution addresses the specific problem situations mapped, and
  every solution element traces to a behaviour or a constraint.
- Top three to five assumptions that feel genuinely uncomfortable — if any of them is wrong,
  the canvas collapses. An assumption set that is comfortable has not found the real risk.
- A synthesis of roughly 250 words naming what was established, what remains a gap, and what
  the canvas hands to the next framework.

## Builds on / feeds

Builds on Working Backwards (customer, problem, and solution narratives, plus the internal
FAQ's assumptions) and Jobs-to-be-Done (job statements, struggling moments, current
alternatives); takes available solutions, alternatives, and constraints from Competitive
Landscape. Owns the solution description and its fit validation: Lean Canvas references it
for the Solution block and takes Problem, Customer Segments, and a partial value proposition
from it; Five Whys takes the refined problem statement as its starting point; the validation
milestone's Leap of Faith takes the tagged critical assumptions.
