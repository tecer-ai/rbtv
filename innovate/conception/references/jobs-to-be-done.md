# Jobs-to-be-Done

People do not buy products; in a specific situation they **hire** whichever option best
advances them, including spreadsheets, email, and doing nothing. Jobs-to-be-Done names that
progress. Use it after Working Backwards, to replace a demographic description of the
customer with the concrete situation in which they reach for a solution, and to learn what
they are really comparing against.

## The sequence

Worked in this order, always. Each step consumes the previous one's output, so a skipped or
reordered step is not a shortcut — it silently invalidates everything downstream of it.

| # | Step | What it produces | What breaks if it is skipped or taken out of order |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Draft job hypotheses | Two or three candidate job statements — "When [situation], I want to [progress], so I can [outcome]" — explicitly labeled as untested guesses | There is nothing specific to test, so the interviews wander and produce anecdotes instead of evidence for or against a claim |
| 2 | Recruit the three groups | Named recent adopters, switchers and strugglers to interview | Only happy existing users get heard, and the strugglers who reveal the real alternative — spreadsheets, email, doing nothing — never enter the model |
| 3 | Run the timeline interviews | Concrete past episodes walked end to end (open → trigger → passive looking → active looking → deciding → consuming), with the emotional, social and stakes probes asked in each | Hypotheticals replace episodes; every later step inherits what people say they would do rather than what they did |
| 4 | Rewrite the job statements from evidence | Job stories in customer language, each carrying real quotes, with the main job separated from related ones | Step 1's guesses survive to the end wearing the authority of research |
| 5 | Split each job into its three types | The functional, emotional and social job stated separately for each job | Only the functional job gets captured, which is the single most common way this framework produces a shallow result |
| 6 | Run the forces analysis | Push, pull, anxieties and habits — why people switch AND why they stay | The model explains attraction but not resistance, so adoption blockers stay invisible until launch |
| 7 | Build the job map | The stages Define → Locate → Prepare → Execute → Confirm → Evolve, each with its own pain points | Pain is attributed to the product as a whole instead of to a stage, and there is nowhere specific for a solution to attach |
| 8 | Prioritize and select | Every job scored on importance, current satisfaction and strategic fit, with ONE primary job chosen and the secondary jobs marked as not yet optimized for | The milestone carries several unranked jobs forward, and Problem-Solution Fit and Lean Canvas each pick a different one |
| 9 | Check against Working Backwards | A statement of which press-release claims the job evidence supports and which need revision | The earlier customer story and this one diverge quietly, and both stay in the project as if they agreed |

Steps 2 and 3 may overlap in practice — recruiting continues while early interviews run. Nothing
else moves.

## The structure

**Job statement.** "When [situation or trigger], I want to [progress], so I can [ultimate
outcome]."

**Three job types**, all three present in every hiring decision:

| Type | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| Functional | What they are doing | "Get accurate data to stakeholders" |
| Emotional | How they want to feel | "Feel in control of my team's output" |
| Social | How they want to be perceived | "Look competent to my leadership" |

**The four forces.** Adoption happens when Push + Pull outweigh Anxieties + Habits.

| Force | Direction | The question it answers |
|---|---|---|
| Push of the present | Away from the status quo | What is painful about the current way? |
| Pull of the new | Toward the new solution | What is attractive about the alternative? |
| Anxieties | Resisting change | What fears exist about adopting something new? |
| Habits | Inertia | What comfort exists in the status quo? |

**Job map** — the stages a digital-product job typically passes through, each with its own
pain points: Define (recognize and frame the problem) → Locate (find options and information)
→ Prepare (set up data, integrations, processes) → Execute (do the core work) → Confirm
(check results, share with stakeholders) → Evolve (adjust and refine).

## The interview method

Three groups worth interviewing: **recent adopters** (started with your or a competing
solution in the last three to six months), **switchers** (moved from one solution to
another), and **strugglers** (still on workarounds — spreadsheets, email, manual process).

Anchor every interview on one concrete past episode, never a hypothetical: open ("tell me
about the last time you [situation] — when was that?"), trigger ("what changed that made this
worth addressing?"), passive looking ("what did you try casually at first?"), active looking
("when did you decide you needed a better way, and what did you search for?"), deciding ("what
made you choose X over Y or over doing nothing?"), consuming ("walk me through the first few
times you used it").

Probe the other two dimensions explicitly: "how did you feel at that moment?" (emotional),
"who else cared whether this worked?" (social), "what would have happened if you had done
nothing?" (stakes).

## What a good output contains

- Job hypotheses that can be explained without mentioning the product, that carry a concrete
  situation trigger rather than a demographic, and for which three or more real people or
  organizations can be named.
- Job stories supported by real interview quotes, written in customer language rather than in
  the shape of a user interface, distinguishing the main job from related, emotional, and
  social jobs.
- A forces analysis that explains both why people switch AND why they stay, naming the
  specific anxieties blocking adoption and the habits creating inertia.
- A prioritization of every job on importance, current satisfaction, and strategic fit —
  high importance plus low satisfaction is the attractive quadrant — resulting in ONE primary
  job selected for this milestone, with any secondary jobs explicitly marked as not being
  optimized for yet.
- A consistency check against Working Backwards: which press-release claims the job evidence
  supports, and which need revision.

## Pitfalls

Confusing personas or features with jobs — a job is progress, not a demographic and not a
button. Capturing only functional jobs. Relying on hypotheticals instead of specific past
episodes. Over-fitting to early adopters without testing whether the job appears in
mainstream segments. Treating the analysis as one-time rather than revisiting it when
adoption patterns shift.

## Builds on / feeds

Builds on Working Backwards for the customer and problem it starts from. Owns the customer
jobs and the hiring/firing criteria: Problem-Solution Fit references the jobs when mapping
solution fit, and Lean Canvas draws its Problem, Customer Segments, and value-proposition
blocks from the job forces and job owners.
